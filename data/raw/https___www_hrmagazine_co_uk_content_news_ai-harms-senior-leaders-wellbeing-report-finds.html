    AI harms senior leaders’ wellbeing, report finds

    More than eight in 10 (86%) senior leaders report that using large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT has negatively impacted their wellbeing, according to research findings published by enterprise AI platform UnlikelyAI today (17 March).

    After surveying 1,000 senior decision-makers, The AI Trust Report 2026 revealed that almost one in three experience burnout (32%) and analysis paralysis (31%) from using LLMs in the workplace.

    The report also highlighted that 87% say they trust AI outputs, but 99% still check them. More than half (53%) of respondents noted that employees spend as much time checking AI as using it.

    James Devine, senior partner at business consultancy Korn Ferry, told HR magazine that he was not surprised by these findings.

    He said: “Many organisations have adopted AI at speed, but the human implications of that shift have not always been addressed with the same urgency.

    “Senior leaders are under pressure to understand a rapidly evolving technology, make strategic decisions about it, and deliver productivity gains, mostly without clear guardrails or sufficient organisational capability.”

    Read more: Two thirds of HR have ‘little energy’ for their own wellbeing

    Sara Gutierrez, chief science officer at recruitment assessment provider SHL, agreed that many leaders are navigating a period of rapid technological change.

    Gutierrez told HR magazine: “Effective AI leadership is not just about technical literacy; it also relies heavily on human capabilities such as critical thinking, judgement, adaptability and confidence in decision-making.”

    According to The AI Trust Report 2026, two thirds of leaders (65%) feel anxious knowing their AI-assisted work is being seen by others.

    To improve leaders' wellbeing, Gutierrez advised HR professionals to assist in building peer learning communities, and to implement clearer governance around AI use.

    Devine added: “HR should treat AI change like any other major transformation, [and offer] structured support.

    “This includes targeted training, access to trusted internal expertise, and wellbeing check-ins that acknowledge the mental load of navigating technological disruption. AI may be a technical shift, but its success will ultimately depend on human sustainability.”

    Almost a third (30%) of the leaders surveyed experience AI blindness, which UnlikelyAI defined as when someone loses perspective on what looks good after too many AI prompts.

    Read more: How can HR tackle the sharp rise in AI-generated grievances?

    Speaking to HR magazine, communication and emotional regulation consultant Ayesha Dornelly said: “AI often accelerates decision-making expectations without reducing the responsibility leaders carry for those decisions. 

    “Many feel pressure to move faster because the technology appears fast, even though the judgement required to use it responsibly still takes time.”

    Frances Cushway, founder and managing director of The Maternity Coach consultancy, told HR magazine: “AI works best when people know how it can best support their work, so providing training in how to use it will help employees know how and when to use it, and when to stop.”

    Representatives of UnlikelyAI commissioned Censuswide to poll 1,000 senior decision-makers in regulated industries: finance, healthcare, insurance, energy/utilities and the public sector, with budget or decision authority for AI/analytics. Respondents were from organisations that have at least 250 employees. The survey began on 26 November and ended on 8 December 2025.
